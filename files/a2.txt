MASTER PROMPT: Vendor-Agnostic Real-Time Rendering Engine

You are a senior real-time graphics architect and engine programmer. Design and implement a modern vendor-agnostic real-time rendering engine focused on stable image quality, predictable performance, and practical asset workflows. The engine should avoid dependence on vendor-locked ray tracing, blurry temporal anti-aliasing, noisy shadowing, excessive micropolygon geometry, and wasteful material/shader passes.

Primary Goal

Create a rendering engine architecture suitable for modern PC and console-style hardware that can produce clean, realistic, temporally stable images at 60 FPS or higher on mid-range GPUs. Prioritize clear pixels, efficient geometry, robust visibility, and scalable lighting/reflection systems over marketing features.

Core Design Principles

1. Vendor agnostic first:
   - Do not require Nvidia, AMD, or Intel exclusive features.
   - Use standard graphics APIs and broadly supported GPU features.
   - Hardware ray tracing may be optional, but the engine must have a strong compute-shader/software ray tracing path.
   - Prefer Shader Model 5+ compatible compute approaches where practical.

2. Stable image quality:
   - Avoid relying on heavy blurry TAA to hide noise, shimmer, dithering, bad shadows, or unstable GI.
   - Use temporal accumulation only where it improves stability without destroying detail.
   - Support strong non-blurry AA options such as SMAA, SMAA T2x, MSAA hybrids, specular anti-aliasing, filtered mipmaps, and modern rejection logic.
   - Preserve texture detail and motion clarity.

3. Efficient geometry instead of brute force:
   - Do not build the renderer around micropolygon or "infinite detail" assumptions.
   - Use authored or automated LODs with smooth transitions.
   - Avoid sub-pixel triangle density that creates aliasing and forces blur.
   - Prefer optimized vertex-shader paths where they outperform meshlet/micropolygon paths.
   - Provide tools for LOD generation, triangle-density analysis, overdraw analysis, and asset warnings.

4. Practical optimization over visual gimmicks:
   - Every major render feature must have measurable cost, quality mode tiers, debug views, and fallback paths.
   - Avoid full-screen work when tile, stencil, scissor, clustered, or partial methods can reduce cost.
   - Avoid unnecessary clears, copies, texture fetches, and redundant render targets.
   - Prioritize stable frame time over peak screenshot quality.

Required Renderer Architecture

Build a deferred or hybrid deferred renderer with the following pipeline:

1. Frame setup:
   - Clear only resources that need clearing.
   - Track transient render targets and alias memory safely.
   - Build per-frame camera, exposure, motion vector, culling, and light data.

2. Visibility and prepass:
   - Implement a partial depth prepass for large opaque occluders and expensive materials.
   - Use front-to-back sorting where useful.
   - Keep the prepass depth-focused and fast.
   - Do not calculate unnecessary pixel-shader data in the prepass unless required.
   - Generate Hi-Z depth for occlusion, SSR, RT culling, decals, and light culling.
   - Exclude alpha-tested foliage and dense masked content from the prepass unless profiling proves a benefit.

3. Geometry/base pass:
   - Write compact, well-designed G-buffers.
   - Store normals with sufficient precision, such as RGB10A2 or another quality-conscious packing format.
   - Avoid albedo compression schemes that corrupt MSAA/subsample edges.
   - Generate motion vectors correctly for static, skinned, animated, instanced, foliage, and camera-driven movement.
   - Keep material texture counts low and expose warnings for high-cost materials.
   - Support baked material packing and object-specific texture baking for complex assets.

4. Materials and textures:
   - Prefer efficient compressed formats and channel packing.
   - Avoid wasteful high-resolution atlases when smaller mip-friendly textures are enough.
   - Avoid stochastic virtual texture blending that creates visible noise unless a non-noisy method is implemented.
   - Provide automatic material cost reports based on texture count, texture format, overdraw, and shaded pixel area.

5. Decals:
   - Support efficient mesh decals or clipped decal geometry where possible.
   - Avoid forcing a full prepass only to support projected decals.
   - Provide projected decals as a fallback, not as the only path.

6. Shadows:
   - Implement cached shadow maps for static or mostly static lights.
   - Use conventional shadow atlases, stable cascades, and per-light caching before considering complex virtual shadow methods.
   - Exclude floors or other low-value receivers from expensive shadow passes when safe.
   - Use stencil, scissor, tile, or light-volume restrictions to reduce shadow projection cost.
   - Avoid noise-dependent shadows that require blurry TAA to look acceptable.
   - Provide debug views for shadow caster cost, receiver cost, cache invalidation, atlas occupancy, and per-light timing.

7. Direct and indirect lighting:
   - Use a physically plausible lighting model; avoid Lambert-only diffuse as the default for realistic materials.
   - Implement Burley diffuse or another higher-quality diffuse model where appropriate.
   - Support tiled, clustered, or light-volume deferred lighting and compare performance.
   - Implement scalable ambient occlusion that is stable, depth-aware, and not destructively noisy.
   - For GI, prioritize stability, low smear, and graceful fallback over noisy full-scene tracing.

8. Reflections and ray tracing:
   - Implement a reflection stack:
     - Screen space reflections first for visible on-screen detail.
     - Compute-shader/software ray tracing for world-space fallback.
     - Optional hardware RT path only as an enhancement.
     - Cubemaps only as a low-priority fallback for rough or distant surfaces.
   - Trace at adaptive resolution, commonly half resolution or checkerboard-like coverage when needed.
   - Use blue-noise or carefully designed sampling where it improves convergence.
   - Use temporal reconstruction with strong rejection, motion vectors, roughness awareness, and depth/normal validation.
   - Represent large world-space occluders in a simplified acceleration structure.
   - Use simplified vertex-colored proxy geometry for reflection tracing where possible.
   - Include emissive objects in world-space reflection representation even when small.
   - Support special handling for glass and transparent reflections, including separate interior/exterior reflection layers if useful.

9. Anti-aliasing and reconstruction:
   - Implement SMAA and SMAA T2x as primary low-cost options.
   - Explore a modern SMAA 4x-like hybrid using 2x MSAA plus temporal reprojection.
   - Support MSAA in deferred/hybrid paths with correct sample-frequency shading where needed.
   - Resolve MSAA with proper edge masks, linear blending, and correct handling of AO/specular/subsample data.
   - Add specular anti-aliasing through filtered mipmaps, normal filtering, roughness adjustment, and material authoring rules.
   - Avoid temporal AA that smears particles, lighting, faces, thin geometry, or camera motion.

10. Post-processing:
   - Use motion blur only when high quality and natural; make it optional.
   - Keep bloom, depth of field, fog, film grain, chromatic aberration, sharpening, and color grading modular.
   - Avoid forced sharpening that creates halos or worsens aliasing.
   - Prefer stable blue-noise patterns over obvious interleaved gradient noise in fog or volumetrics.
   - Ensure post effects do not corrupt alpha, motion, or temporal history buffers.

11. Debugging and profiling:
   - Provide frame graph visualization.
   - Provide GPU timestamp scopes for every pass.
   - Provide per-draw and per-material cost views.
   - Provide overdraw heatmaps that distinguish hardware raster, compute raster, masked overdraw, and material overdraw.
   - Provide G-buffer, depth, normals, motion vectors, shadow atlas, Hi-Z, SSR, RT, lighting, AO, AA, and history debug views.
   - Provide warnings for expensive materials, excessive texture fetches, sub-pixel triangles, unstable temporal history, unnecessary clears/copies, and poor shadow cache behavior.

Performance Targets

Design the engine around these rough frame-budget goals at 1080p on a mid-range GPU:

- Geometry and base pass: 2.0 to 4.0 ms depending on scene complexity.
- Shadows: below 1.5 ms for common scenes, with caching for static lights.
- Reflections: 1.0 to 2.5 ms depending on quality tier.
- AO/GI: scalable, stable, and preferably below 2.0 ms at performance settings.
- Anti-aliasing: preferably below 1.0 ms for the default path.
- Post-processing: below 1.5 ms unless cinematic quality is requested.
- Total GPU frame: target 16.6 ms or less for 60 FPS, with quality tiers for 8.3 ms and 11.1 ms targets.

Implementation Deliverables

Produce:

1. A complete renderer architecture document.
2. A frame graph showing all passes, inputs, outputs, and resource lifetimes.
3. Data structures for scene objects, lights, materials, render targets, acceleration structures, and temporal history.
4. Pseudocode or real code for:
   - Partial depth prepass.
   - G-buffer/base pass.
   - Hi-Z generation.
   - Light culling.
   - Shadow caching and projection.
   - SSR.
   - Compute-shader/software ray traced reflections.
   - Temporal reflection reconstruction.
   - SMAA/SMAA T2x or MSAA hybrid resolve.
   - Debug view generation.
5. A scalability system with Low, Medium, High, and Cinematic settings.
6. A profiling and validation checklist.

Important Constraints

- Do not design around heavy TAA as a universal fix.
- Do not require hardware RT.
- Do not require Nanite-style micropolygon rendering.
- Do not require virtual shadow maps.
- Do not require stochastic virtual textures.
- Do not hide artifacts with blur, grain, chromatic aberration, or aggressive sharpening.
- Do not accept features without clear debug views and measurable frame cost.

Final Output Style

When answering, be concrete and engineering-focused. Give the architecture first, then the pipeline, then the data structures, then pseudocode, then optimization/debug tooling. Explain tradeoffs clearly and include fallback paths for weaker hardware.
